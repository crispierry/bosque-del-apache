# Photography Practice Techniques

## Goal

Prepare for Bosque del Apache by practicing the specific camera handling, exposure, autofocus, composition, and field workflow techniques most likely to matter for early December bird and wetland photography.

## Website Section

Add this content as a top-level `Practice` workspace in the website, next to `Gear`.

Primary use:

- Choose a technique to practice before the trip.
- Tie each drill to a Bosque field scenario.
- Track confidence and recurring mistakes.
- Move proven settings into the gear checklist before departure.

## Technique Card Format

Each practice item should use this structure:

- Technique.
- Bosque scenario.
- Why it matters.
- Starting setup.
- Drill.
- Success standard.
- Common misses.
- Home practice location.
- Gear.
- Notes to carry into the field.

## Priority Drills

### Birds-In-Flight Acquisition

- Bosque scenario: cranes or geese lifting off before sunrise, crossing the wetland, or landing in groups.
- Why it matters: the hardest moment is often the first second after a bird enters the frame.
- Starting setup: continuous autofocus, subject detection if reliable, high-speed burst, long lens wide enough to find the bird quickly.
- Drill: practice raising the camera, finding a moving bird or distant object, tracking for three seconds, and firing a short burst only when framing is clean.
- Success standard: acquire the subject without hunting and keep the head or body in focus through the burst.
- Common misses: starting too tight, chasing the subject after it leaves the autofocus area, and holding the shutter too long.
- Home practice location: local park, shoreline, athletic field, street with predictable moving subjects.
- Gear: the longest lens expected for the trip, plus the camera strap or support setup planned for the refuge.
- Field note: begin slightly wider than maximum reach during chaotic fly-outs, then zoom tighter once tracking is stable.

### Panning Ladder

- Bosque scenario: lateral crane flight, geese crossing low over water, and slower wingbeat silhouettes at sunrise or sunset.
- Why it matters: smooth panning keeps motion graceful instead of random.
- Starting setup: shutter priority or manual exposure, continuous autofocus, burst mode.
- Drill: photograph moving subjects at 1/2000, 1/1000, 1/500, 1/250, and 1/125 second.
- Success standard: know which shutter speeds freeze detail, which show controlled wing blur, and where the keeper rate collapses.
- Common misses: stopping the pan when pressing the shutter, clipping wings, and using a stance that twists at the shoulders.
- Home practice location: birds, cyclists, cars on a quiet street, runners, or dogs at a park.
- Gear: handheld long lens, monopod, or tripod/gimbal if that is part of the planned kit.
- Field note: use faster speeds for identification and behavior; slow down intentionally only when trying for expressive motion.

### Takeoff And Landing Anticipation

- Bosque scenario: sandhill cranes preparing to launch, geese bunching before a blast-off, or birds dropping into shallow water.
- Why it matters: the strongest frame often happens before the obvious peak action.
- Starting setup: continuous autofocus, burst mode, enough shutter speed for wing motion, and a pre-selected zone with clean background.
- Drill: watch birds or moving subjects without shooting for two minutes, call the likely movement, then shoot only when the prediction starts to happen.
- Success standard: capture the start of motion, not only the aftermath.
- Common misses: reacting after the flock has already lifted, ignoring body language, and letting the background get busy.
- Home practice location: park pond, shorebirds, gulls, ducks, sports warmups.
- Gear: long wildlife lens, optional teleconverter only after acquisition feels reliable.
- Field note: at Bosque, practice watching posture, flock tension, wind direction, and birds turning into the wind.

### White Bird And Bright Water Exposure

- Bosque scenario: snow geese in sun, white birds over reflective water, and pale birds against dark vegetation.
- Why it matters: white feathers can lose detail quickly, especially when the background is darker.
- Starting setup: exposure compensation available on a dial, highlight warning enabled, raw capture.
- Drill: photograph white or bright subjects against dark, neutral, and bright backgrounds, changing exposure compensation in one-third stop increments.
- Success standard: preserve feather or highlight detail while keeping the scene usable.
- Common misses: trusting evaluative metering blindly, overcorrecting too dark, and forgetting to reset compensation.
- Home practice location: white signs, gulls, egrets, white vehicles, sunlit water, or pale objects in open shade.
- Gear: any camera/lens combination, but repeat with the long lens before the trip.
- Field note: review highlights after the first burst of each new light situation.

### Backlit Silhouette Control

- Bosque scenario: cranes and geese against sunrise color, sunset fly-ins, and rim-lit birds over water.
- Why it matters: silhouette frames are a major Bosque opportunity, but they need decisive exposure.
- Starting setup: manual exposure or exposure compensation, highlight warning, stable white balance.
- Drill: expose for the bright sky, then make variations from pure silhouette to slight shadow detail.
- Success standard: preserve sky color and keep the bird shape readable.
- Common misses: lifting exposure until the sky washes out, placing the bird over a cluttered background, and using too slow a shutter.
- Home practice location: sunrise/sunset sky, backlit trees, birds on wires, or moving subjects against bright backgrounds.
- Gear: long lens for birds; 24-105mm for wider environmental silhouettes.
- Field note: look for clean edges around neck, legs, wings, and flock shapes.

### Manual Exposure For Stable Light

- Bosque scenario: pre-dawn fly-out, sunrise color, and sunset fly-in where backgrounds change faster than the light on the subject.
- Why it matters: automatic metering can jump as birds pass over water, reeds, sky, or mountains.
- Starting setup: manual exposure, auto ISO optional if tested, histogram/highlight review.
- Drill: set exposure from one test frame, then shoot moving subjects against different backgrounds without changing exposure.
- Success standard: the subject stays consistently exposed across changing backgrounds.
- Common misses: adjusting exposure for every background, letting auto ISO behave unpredictably, and forgetting to update as light rises.
- Home practice location: any moving subject crossing sky, trees, pavement, and water.
- Gear: primary wildlife camera and long lens.
- Field note: update exposure when the light changes, not every time the background changes.

### Long-Lens Support Rehearsal

- Bosque scenario: photographing from road edges, vehicle pullouts, overlooks, and possibly long waits in cold air.
- Why it matters: fatigue and awkward support can quietly wreck sharpness.
- Starting setup: test handheld, monopod, tripod, beanbag, and vehicle-window support if available.
- Drill: make ten-frame bursts from each support method at several shutter speeds, then compare sharpness at 100%.
- Success standard: know the slowest reliable shutter speed for each support method.
- Common misses: leaning into the lens, poor foot position, loose tripod head, and forgetting stabilization mode differences.
- Home practice location: backyard, balcony, parked car, local overlook.
- Gear: 200-600mm, 400-800mm, 100-500mm, 200-800mm, and teleconverter combinations under consideration.
- Field note: choose support based on the shot window, not on habit.

### Fast Focal-Length Decisions

- Bosque scenario: a bird approaches too close for 800mm, a flock fills the sky, or the landscape becomes more important than an individual bird.
- Why it matters: Bosque can switch from intimate wildlife to big environmental scenes in seconds.
- Starting setup: one long lens mounted, one wider lens ready, and a clear rule for when to switch or zoom out.
- Drill: alternate between tight subject framing and wider environmental framing every two minutes.
- Success standard: change framing without losing track of the subject or missing the next action.
- Common misses: staying too tight, changing lenses in blowing dust, and ignoring the wider scene.
- Home practice location: park, wetland, overlook, or any place with moving subjects and a meaningful background.
- Gear: long wildlife zoom plus 24-105mm.
- Field note: if the sky, water, or mountains are doing something beautiful, make at least one wider frame before returning to reach.

### Reflection And Foreground Composition

- Bosque scenario: calm water, dawn color, cranes standing in pools, and layered wetland habitat.
- Why it matters: not every strong image will be high-action flight.
- Starting setup: aperture priority or manual exposure, single-point or flexible autofocus area, level display enabled.
- Drill: make compositions where the subject, reflection, foreground reeds, and horizon each get deliberate placement.
- Success standard: produce frames that still read clearly when the bird is small in the image.
- Common misses: cutting off reflections, placing horizons accidentally, and letting foreground clutter touch the subject.
- Home practice location: pond, river, puddles, lakeshore, or reflective urban surfaces.
- Gear: 24-105mm and long zoom.
- Field note: simplify the frame before adding more habitat.

### Cold-Weather Field Workflow

- Bosque scenario: pre-dawn setup, battery drain, gloves, lens fog, dust, and fast transitions between car and field position.
- Why it matters: missed shots often come from friction outside the camera settings.
- Starting setup: full field kit packed exactly as planned.
- Drill: do a timed sunrise simulation: dress, pack, mount lens, set exposure, make test frame, check highlights, and reset for travel.
- Success standard: be ready to shoot within two minutes of parking.
- Common misses: buried gloves, dead battery in camera, missing card, loose plate, and lens cloth not reachable.
- Home practice location: driveway, balcony, or local park before sunrise.
- Gear: full trip kit, including clothing and bag.
- Field note: the checklist should be physical or phone-accessible without digging through the bag.

## Six-Week Practice Cadence

### Six Weeks Out

- Confirm camera button layout for autofocus, burst, exposure compensation, and review.
- Practice birds-in-flight acquisition twice.
- Test long-lens support methods.

### Five Weeks Out

- Run the panning ladder twice.
- Practice takeoff and landing anticipation at a local bird location.
- Review misses at 100% and write one correction.

### Four Weeks Out

- Practice exposure compensation on white subjects and bright water.
- Practice manual exposure across changing backgrounds.
- Decide when auto ISO helps or hurts.

### Three Weeks Out

- Practice silhouettes at sunrise or sunset.
- Practice reflection and foreground compositions.
- Build a short shot list for non-flight scenes.

### Two Weeks Out

- Run a full sunrise simulation with cold-weather clothing.
- Practice fast focal-length decisions with the exact lens pair planned for the trip.
- Finalize support choice for vehicle, roadside, and walking situations.

### One Week Out

- Rehearse the pack-tonight checklist.
- Clean sensors/lenses or schedule service if needed.
- Lock in baseline camera settings.
- Create a one-page field reference for exposure, autofocus, and support.
